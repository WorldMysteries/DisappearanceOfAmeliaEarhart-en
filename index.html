The Disappearance of Amelia Earhart

The last dawn over the Pacific, July 2, 1937

In the twentieth hour of her nonstop flight from New Guinea to the tiny coral island of Howland lost in the middle of the Pacific, Amelia Earhart's voice burst into the radio room of the US Coast Guard cutter «Itasca» at maximum strength: «We must be on you, but we cannot see you — gas is running low.» An hour later, the twin-engine Lockheed Electra with Earhart and navigator Fred Noonan dissolved forever between sky and water, leaving the greatest mystery in the history of world aviation.

Sheet No. 02 • Radio room transcript of USCGC «Itasca»

The morning of July 2, 1937: voices in a one-way air

The US Coast Guard cutter «Itasca» awaited the aircraft off the western shore of Howland Island. Chief radioman Leo Bellarts heard Amelia Earhart's voice at thunderous volume, but due to a fatal mismatch of frequencies and antennas the crew of the Electra was flying practically blind.

Anatomy of the radio trap

Three reasons for loss of contact at the target

1. Incompatible direction finders. Earhart transmitted voice on 3105 kHz, while «Itasca»'s marine DF set operated only on long waves up to 550 kHz.

2. The DF loop's blindness on 7500 kHz. When «Itasca» replied on 7500 kHz, the airborne loop antenna of the Electra physically could not produce an angular null on such a short wave.

3. A 30-minute time-zone offset. The ship kept watch on ship's time GMT−11:30, while the crew used Greenwich time, so receiving and transmitting sessions overlapped.

A fatal planning error: Earhart whistles into the microphone for just a few seconds, unaware that the experimental US Navy high-frequency DF set on Howland has dead batteries and cannot catch such a short transmission, while the shipboard DF of «Itasca» works only up to 550 kHz.

The signal strengthens rapidly, but communication remains entirely one-way. Earhart transmits strictly at :15 and :45 of each hour according to her own schedule, yet for some reason does not hear the powerful transmitter of «Itasca» — probably because of the mismatch between ship time (GMT−11:30) and the Greenwich scale.

«We must be right over you but cannot see you — fuel is running low»

«KHAQQ calling Itasca. We must be on you but cannot see you — gas is running low. Been unable to reach you by radio. We are flying at 1,000 feet.» “KHAQQ calling Itasca. We must be on you but cannot see you — gas is running low. Been unable to reach you by radio. We are flying at 1,000 feet.”

Earhart's voice thunders in the radio operators' headphones so loudly (S5 out of 5) as if the aircraft were just beyond the horizon — within 25–60 miles. «Itasca»'s commander Warner Thompson orders the oil boilers forced, raising a multi-kilometer column of thick black smoke over the ocean, but the horizon is empty.

07:58 20:28 GMT 3105 ←→ 7500 kHz S5 • maximum strength
Amelia Earhart → «Itasca» • the only two-way contact

Earhart hears «Itasca» on 7500 kHz for the first time, but cannot determine direction

«KHAQQ calling Itasca. We are receiving your signals, but unable to get a minimum. Please take bearing on us and answer 3105 with voice.» “KHAQQ calling Itasca. We are receiving your signals, but unable to get a minimum. Please take bearing on us and answer 3105 with voice.”

A physical trap: the Bendix loop antenna on the cabin roof of the Electra was designed for 200–1500 kHz. On the short 7500 kHz wave the wavelength is too small, so rotating the loop produced no «null minimum» — the «Itasca» signal sounded equally loud from all directions.

08:43 21:13 GMT 3105 kHz S5 • very loud, cut off
Amelia Earhart → «Itasca» • last signal in the air

Last confirmed transmission: «We are on the line 157–337... running north and south»

«KHAQQ to Itasca: We are on the line 157 337. Will repeat this message. Will repeat this on 6210 kilocycles. Wait.» [after a few seconds, again on 3105 kHz]: «We are running on line north and south.» “KHAQQ to Itasca: We are on the line 157 337. Will repeat this message. Will repeat this on 6210 kilocycles. Wait... We are running on line north and south.”

Extreme tension is audible in Earhart's voice. Noonan has computed from the morning sun's altitude (azimuth 67° NE) a celestial line of position perpendicular to 157°–337°. Knowing the line passes through Howland but not whether they are north or south of the island, the crew began a desperate search along it up and down. The transmission on 6210 kHz never came.

10:40 (Jul 2) – Jul 18 23:10 GMT+ 3105 / 6210 kHz disputed post-crash signals
USCGC «Itasca», battleship «Colorado», carrier «Lexington»

Massive search across 250,000 sq. miles and the phenomenon of nighttime radio intercepts

Logs of Howland, Baker, Honolulu, and amateur radio stations (July 2–6): faint carrier waves on 3105 kHz and fragments of words «...KHAQQ... water... help...» only during low tide. Carrier waves and fragmented voice calls logged on 3105 kHz and harmonic 6210 kHz across July 2–6 before falling permanently silent.

If the Electra sank in the ocean, the transmitter died instantly. Yet for 4 days Pan American stations (capable of taking bearings) recorded faint signals on 3105 kHz whose crossings converged in the Phoenix Islands area. The aircraft could only have transmitted from the ground on the reef during low tide, starting the right engine with its generator. On July 19 President Roosevelt officially ended the search.

Unlike the standard passenger Electras (10A, 450 hp each), the 10E variant was powered by high-altitude Wasp S3H1 engines of 550 hp and Hamilton Standard constant-speed propellers. The right engine drove the sole onboard 50-ampere generator.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937During a forced landing on a coral reef, the spinning right propeller would have allowed the radio's batteries to be recharged until the tidal water reached the propeller hub.
NODE B-02Six auxiliary fuselage fuel tanksConverted from 10-seat cabin

Ten passenger seats were removed, and almost the whole cabin volume was occupied by huge aluminum fuel tanks. Only a narrow crawlway over the tanks connected the cockpit to Fred Noonan's navigation table — the pilot and navigator had to pass notes on a bamboo pole with a clothespin.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937The lack of direct voice and visual contact between Earhart and Noonan in the critical minutes of the Howland search sharply slowed the exchange of coordinates and frequency changes.
NODE C-03Bendix RA-1 loop radio direction finder (above the cabin)Incompatible with 7500 kHz
OPERATING DF RANGE:200 – 1,430 kHz (effective)

A metal loop above the cabin allowed the pilot to rotate the antenna until the signal's «null minimum» disappeared, determining the exact bearing to the station. However, the receiver with its new coupler had five switchable bands and worked poorly above 1,500 kHz.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937At 07:58 that morning Earhart heard «Itasca»'s transmission on 7500 kHz, but the wavelength (40 meters) caused resonance with the metal fuselage — the direction finder gave no null («unable to get a minimum»), robbing the crew of a life-saving compass vector.
NODE D-04Upper V-shaped transmitting antenna and the removed 500 kHz trailing wireLoss of the marine DF channel
REMOVED IN MIAMI:76-meter trailing antenna, 500 kHz

The top antenna wire stretched from the mast above the cabin to the two tail fins. The international maritime distress and DF frequency of 500 kHz required unreeling a 76-meter copper wire from the tail in flight. Tired of hand-reeling it, Earhart left the long-wave equipment behind on the ground.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937The standard marine radio direction finder of the cutter «Itasca» worked perfectly on exactly 500 kHz! Had the trailing antenna been aboard, «Itasca»'s radio operators would have given Earhart an exact course to the island in 30 seconds.
NODE E-05Lower receiving wire antenna under the fuselageLikely torn off during takeoff at Lae
CLEARANCE AT FULL LOAD:less than 35 cm

The Bendix receiver was connected to the lower wire antenna beneath the fuselage. Eyewitnesses at the dirt airfield of Lae (and analysis of the newsreel of the final takeoff) pointed to the sagging of the heavily loaded aircraft on the bumps and a possible puff of dust from a broken wire.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937If the lower receiving antenna broke during the takeoff run at Lae, this fully explains why Earhart's transmitter (upper V-antenna) thundered into «Itasca» at 5 by 5, yet she herself heard almost nothing in reply for hours.
NODE F-06Fred Noonan's navigator compartment and astro hatchComputed the 157°–337° line
1937 CELESTIAL NAVIGATION ACCURACY:±10–25 nautical miles after 18 h of flight

Behind the fuel tanks, at a small cabin window, lay the table of navigator Fred Noonan — the man who had laid out Pan Am's transpacific air routes. In flight a bubble sextant allowed him to take star and morning sun sights without a visible horizon.

Role in the events of July 2, 1937At dawn a single sun sight gives not a point on the chart but only one straight line of position (157°–337°). To obtain a second intersecting line, one had to wait for noon or catch a radio bearing from «Itasca».
Sheet No. 04 • Historical and forensic examination of hypotheses

Four hypotheses of the disappearance: facts versus legends

For nearly nine decades the fate of crew NR16020 has spawned both serious oceanographic and archaeological expeditions and loud hoaxes. Open each hypothesis and weigh the arguments.

HYPOTHESIS I~60% of historians

Sinking near Howland

Main academic consensus
Geographic attribution35–100 miles NW of Howland (depth 5,200 m)
Authors and expeditionsUS Navy commission, E. Long, Nauticos

After 19–20 hours of struggling with a headwind and the absence of a two-way radio bearing, the aircraft found itself several dozen miles (most likely to the northwest) from the tiny flat island. Shortly after the last transmission at 08:43, both engines stopped from complete fuel exhaustion, and the Electra ditched in the open ocean.

Facts and evidence «FOR»

  1. Earhart's direct words at 07:42: «Gas is running low» — by 08:43 the aircraft had been in the air over 20 hours.
  2. The S5 signal at «Itasca» proves proximity to Howland (no farther than 50–80 miles).
  3. The all-metal machine with empty tanks could have stayed afloat only for minutes.

Counterarguments

  1. Does not explain over 100 radio intercepts on 3105 kHz from July 2 to 6.
  2. Sonar scans of the seabed (Nauticos 2002–2017, the Deep Sea Vision anomaly 2024) found no wreckage.
Final conclusion of the archival examinationThe version most rigorous under Occam's razor: it requires no additional assumption beyond the actual exhaustion of fuel near the island invisible in the sun's glare.
HYPOTHESIS II13 expeditions

Castaways of Nikumaroro

Strong circumstantial basis
Geographic attributionNikumaroro Atoll (Phoenix Islands), 350 miles SE
Authors and expeditionsTIGHAR (R. Gillespie), anthropologist R. Jantz

Having failed to find Howland along the northern segment of the line of position, the crew turned along it southeast toward the Phoenix Islands. Two to two and a half hours later, on the last drops of fuel, Earhart landed the Electra on a wide, smooth, low-tide-dried coral reef of an uninhabited atoll, from which they signaled for help for several nights until the surf washed the aircraft into the abyss.

Facts and evidence «FOR»

  1. The atoll lies exactly on the 157° azimuth from Howland — the literal continuation of Earhart's last phrase.
  2. The schedule of nighttime post-crash signals coincides with low tide at Nikumaroro reef.
  3. In 1940, 13 bones, a 1930s woman's shoe, a sextant box, and a Bénédictine bottle were found; a 2018 reanalysis matched Amelia more closely than 99% of the sample population.

Counterarguments

  1. Was there enough fuel for another 2.5–3 hours of flight after the words «gas is running low»?
  2. On July 9, seaplanes from the battleship «Colorado» flew over the island and noticed neither aircraft nor people.
Final conclusion of the archival examinationThe only alternative theory that rests on the real navigational geometry of the 157°–337° line, on Pan Am radio bearings, and on the 1940 finds documented in the British colonial archive.
HYPOTHESIS IIIdisputed

Japanese captivity (Saipan)

Rejected by Japanese archives
Geographic attributionMili / Jaluit Atoll → Saipan Island
Authors and versionsF. Goerner (1966), M. Campbell, History Channel (2017)

Claims that the off-course aircraft turned northwest toward the Japanese-mandated Marshall Islands, made a forced landing near Mili Atoll, after which Japanese military forces detained Earhart and Noonan as American spies and transported them to Saipan, where they died in captivity.

Facts and evidence «FOR»

  1. Dozens of accounts recorded in the 1944–1960s by Marshall Islanders about a «white woman pilot in men's clothing».
  2. Testimony of 1944 Marines about papers and a burnt twin-engine aircraft they supposedly saw.

Counterarguments

  1. Mili Atoll is almost 800 miles from Howland, whereas at 07:42–08:43 «Itasca» was receiving a signal of near-range S5 strength.
  2. Captured archives of the Imperial Japanese Navy and Foreign Ministry contain no mention whatsoever; moreover, the Japanese survey ship «Kosyu» itself helped the US in the search.
  3. The «photograph on the Jaluit pier» (2017) turned out to have been printed in a Japanese guidebook back in 1935.
Final conclusion of the archival examinationA vivid product of the WWII Pacific atmosphere that does not withstand verification by «Itasca» radiotelemetry or by Japanese state archives.
HYPOTHESIS IV0% credibility

The identity-switch myth

Documentarily disproven
Geographic attributionNew Jersey, USA (the myth of a 1970 book)
Authors and versionsJ. Gervais and J. Klaas («Amelia Earhart Lives», 1970)

A sensational Cold War-era version: the round-the-world flight was cover for aerial photography of Japanese fortifications on orders from President Roosevelt, and after the war the surviving Amelia was secretly brought to the US and lived out her life under the name of the banker Irene Craigmile Bolam.

Facts and evidence «FOR»

  1. The physical resemblance of the elderly Irene Bolam to a 1965 photograph, and the fact that she too held a private pilot's license.

Counterarguments

  1. Bolam filed a $1.5 million libel suit, presenting her biography, birth and marriage certificates from the 1930s.
  2. The publisher McGraw-Hill immediately withdrew the print run; a forensic facial comparison excluded a match.
  3. The civilian Electra carried no reconnaissance aerial cameras, and the route stayed hundreds of miles clear of Japanese waters.
Final conclusion of the archival examinationA classic example of media mythologization of a 20th-century tragedy, definitively refuted by forensic-anthropological examination and documentary evidence.

Sheet No. 05 • Crew of NR16020 and the expedition's legacy

Two over the ocean: the people at the yoke and the sextant

Crew commander • pilotJul 24, 1897 — missing Jul 2, 1937

Amelia Mary Earhart

Native of Atchison, Kansas • FAI license No. 6017 • first woman to fly the Atlantic solo (1932)

By the summer of 1937, the 39-year-old Amelia Earhart already held a unique series of transoceanic records: solo flights Newfoundland–Ireland (1932), Honolulu–Oakland (1935), and Mexico City–New York. A lecturer and aeronautics consultant at Purdue University, the first president of the women pilots' organization «The Ninety-Nines».

Unlike earlier round-the-world flights, which ran in northern latitudes along a shortened arc, Earhart set the goal of circling the Earth as close to the equator as possible — along the route of a full planetary circumference of 47,000 kilometers.

NavigatorApr 4, 1893 — missing Jul 2, 1937

Frederick Joseph Noonan

Chicago native • master mariner • chief navigator of Pan American Airways' Pacific routes

One of the most experienced aerial navigators of the 1930s. Beginning his career as a sailor on square-riggers at the age of 12 and rising to master of a merchant ship, Noonan developed the celestial routes of the famous Martin M-130 «China Clipper» flying boats from San Francisco to Manila.

His professionalism was proven over 35,400 kilometers of the route: he unerringly guided the Electra across the South Atlantic, the deserts of Africa, and the monsoons of India, and at dawn on July 2 he computed an accurate solar line of position of 157°–337° that passed directly through the Howland area.

«Please know that I am aware of the hazards. I want to do it because I want to do it. Women must try to do things as men have tried. When they fail, their failure must be but a challenge to others.»
— From Amelia Earhart's letter to her husband George Palmer Putnam on the eve of a dangerous flight

Modern deep-water searches (1999–2025)

Nauticos expeditions (2002, 2006, 2017): using autonomous underwater vehicles, more than 6,200 square kilometers of seabed have been surveyed west and northwest of Howland Island at depths up to 5,500 meters. The Electra's wreckage has not yet been found, but the steep underwater slopes of the Howland reef itself remain unexplored.

Deep Sea Vision sonar anomaly (2024): the Hugin 6000 vehicle recorded a cross-shaped echo 100 miles west of Howland, resembling the silhouette of a twin-tail aircraft. A follow-up optical dive in November 2024 established that the object is a natural geological rock formation. The mystery of aircraft NR16020 remains unsolved.
